## The loud one and the quiet one

I like offices. I like the coffee-machine conversation that turns into a solution, the workshop where a whiteboard fills up in an hour, and meeting the people I otherwise only see as a tile on a screen. And still, commuting tires me, especially in winter, when the whole train seems to be sneezing and coughing. I do not need to be in the office five days a week. What I need is a reason to be there.

Recently, [Eugene wrote a LinkedIn post](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/eugene-petzer-25956515_sooo-a-week-ago-i-made-a-tough-decision-activity-7507821648901840896-2BuE) about leaving a new job during probation, after years of working mostly from home. In his words, the daily travel at rush hour, an office without calm or quiet spaces, physical access to the building and the office environment were each an "added friction". Each could be managed on its own. Together, they were too much. He ends with a line that has stayed with me:

> When the working environment is suitable for your most vulnerable employees and colleagues, it's suitable for EVERYONE.

That is his view, drawn from his experience. By temperament, I am almost his opposite: I recharge in a full room, he needs a quiet one. His story and mine still lead me to the same place.

Rigid rules are the wrong answer, because people are different, teams are different, and the same team is different from one month to the next. Some weeks need more time together, others less. I can work almost anywhere, and I love the interaction. Others prefer to keep to themselves and need a quiet corner, and the office should offer them one. What all of us share is teamwork, and some of it works best face to face. For people this different to work well together, we have to know each other, and that is what networking and team events are for: they build team spirit. **So the office days for workshops, networking, collaboration and debate should be agreed by each team, and for the rest of the week we should be free to decide where we work.**

## Why rigid rules fail

According to the [Flex Index](https://flexindex.substack.com/p/hot-off-the-press-the-q3-flex-report), the number of required office days kept rising in 2025, to an average of 2.87 a week, while actual home working barely moved. Enforcement can raise attendance, and [some companies report](https://www.cbre.com/insights/reports/2025-americas-office-occupier-sentiment-survey) that it does. But attendance is all it counts: few policies measure whether the day in the office was worth having. A rule that counts days can also make staying home sick feel like a missed quota.

When Amazon announced five office days in 2024, the reasoning was about culture. [Andy Jassy wrote](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/ceo-andy-jassy-latest-update-on-amazon-return-to-office-manager-team-ratio):

> It's easier for our teammates to learn, model, practice, and strengthen our culture

There is a fair point inside this: people do learn from each other in person. But the claim comes without published data, and the evidence on what mandates deliver points the other way. Ding and Ma found no gain in profitability after return-to-office mandates at S&P 500 firms, [as reported by HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/why-do-bosses-want-return-to-office/706544/). A follow-up found [turnover rising by around 14 percent](https://www.hrdive.com/news/rto-mandates-lead-to-brain-drain-attrition/734989/), mostly among senior and highly skilled staff, and a [preprint by Van Dijcke, Gunsilius and Wright](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.04352v2) finds senior staff leaving at Microsoft, SpaceX and Apple. This counts against strict, number-only mandates.

One popular middle ground does not work much better. "Three days a week, pick any" looks flexible, but a [2025 study in the Journal of Organizational Behavior](https://www.washington.edu/news/2025/04/29/qa-hybrid-policies-can-divide-workplaces/) shows that when every person picks their own days, the team splits into subgroups who overlap less. You commute in to sit on video calls with the colleagues who chose Tuesday. In Microsoft's [2022 survey of 20,000 workers](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/hybrid-work-is-just-work), 73 percent said they need a better reason to go in than company expectations, and 73 percent would go in more often if their team were there.

**People come in for people.**

Any fixed count has a second problem: in my experience, some weeks call for much more time together: a project kick-off, a series of workshops, the weeks before a go-live, the first weeks of a new colleague. Other weeks are quiet delivery phases, when the best thing a team can do for each other is let everyone work in peace. A single fixed company-wide count cannot follow each team's rhythm. A team can.

## Knowing each other is the point

People differ in how they work best, and that is why they need to know each other. When I have to analyse a database or work through a document in full concentration, I am faster and more precise at home. When we need to brainstorm, run a workshop or build a team, the room wins, and it wins by a wider margin when people already know each other. During the COVID-19 pandemic, my experienced development colleagues told me they got more focused work done from home. Our new joiners, at the same time, had a hard time finding their place in the team.

So the honest version of "we are more productive at home" is a split: focused work suits many people at home, especially experienced ones, and learning happens near the team. A team with new joiners is the clearest case of needs that change: it comes in more, and while the new joiners are learning, their need should set the minimum, whatever the seniors prefer.

Some teamwork needs a room. Discussion-heavy meetings, such as retrospectives and technical discussions, benefit from people being on site, while information-sharing meetings work well remotely. The session where requirements meet code belongs in the room: walking the developers through a request, the reasons behind it and the options for solving it. Those conversations go better when people know how the others think and argue. Tamas Mezei makes the same point about [global software teams](https://www.brokenrobot.xyz/blog/navigating-cultural-clashes-in-global-software-teams/):

> When people trust each other and have personal rapport, differences in style are more easily forgiven.

That knowledge rarely grows in a video call that starts on the hour and ends five minutes early. It grows when people meet, at networking and team events. Atlassian, a company that has gone distributed, reports from its [own research in 2024](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/distributed-work/intentional-togetherness-research) that planned team gatherings raised team connection by 27 percent on average, an effect that lasted four to five months. [Dropbox reports](https://blog.dropbox.com/topics/company/Virtual-First-2025-Designing-a-culture-that-drives-impact) that its gatherings worked best when they had clear goals. Both are self-reported, by firms with a commercial stake in remote work, but they match my experience.

Team events belong at the centre of the office days. They do not all have to be social: a workshop, a planning day or a lunch with the colleagues from the other office builds team spirit too. The social side has to stay an offer that people can decline freely, and it should vary instead of defaulting to drinks. The frictions Eugene describes add up quickly, and an evening out can be one more. I say that as someone who would happily stay until the end.

## Agreed by the team, built for different people

The team should agree on its office days, and the surveys favour that on fairness and satisfaction. In [BCG's 2023 survey](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2023/flexible-working-models), 24 percent of employees were unhappy with the policy when the company decided where they work, against 6 percent when the team decided. [Gallup found in 2025](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/694361/hybrid-work-retreat-barely.aspx) that 91 percent rated team-decided schedules as fair, against 73 percent for employer-decided ones. Gallup also linked individually chosen schedules to more burnout:

> hybrid work models work best when teams, not individuals, decide the rules.

Neither survey says anything about output. The burnout finding is why the agreed office days matter. Once a team has set them, they bind its members, apart from private adjustments, and the team gets the shared structure that purely individual schedules lack. The freedom starts after that: for the rest of the week, each person decides where to work, which leaves room for the focused work many people do better at home.

The office itself has to work for different people, and the team has to be open to every teammate's needs. Someone like Eugene could work from home most of the week and come in for the workshop or the networking event, with a desk offered for the hours in between, away from the meeting rooms, in a quiet spot. Ideally not next to me, or to any other business analyst who spends the whole day talking in meetings. In Switzerland, trains have quiet compartments where nobody takes a call and nobody expects a chat. Why not the same at work, especially in open-plan offices? Quiet corners like that cost little compared to the desks a mandate fills, and they decide whether some colleagues can take part in the day at all. The need for quiet can have many reasons, and nobody should have to explain theirs. Where someone needs more, such as remote participation by default or a different format for the same purpose, that is a private arrangement between the person and their lead, made without arguing the case in front of the team. I cannot say whether this would have been enough for Eugene. That is his to judge.

For a distributed team, the meeting room matters just as much. My team meets across three or four offices, and I have seen what goes wrong. A whiteboard on the side wall is invisible to everyone outside the room. A camera that shows the whole table makes it hard for remote colleagues to see who is speaking. When one person shares their desktop, only one person has the mouse. Microsoft, for one, documents what helps: a [shared digital whiteboard](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoftteams/rooms/whiteboard-rooms) on a touch screen, so people in the other offices and at home can draw as well, and cameras that frame each speaker, with [several cameras](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoftteams/rooms/room-planning-guidance) in larger rooms. A room like that is a reason to come in, though across offices it narrows the gap rather than closing it, which is why, for a cross-office team, the gatherings matter most.

## Objections, answered

The strongest case against this argument goes like this. New joiners need more than an occasional office day near their team. Real hybrid needs two or three anchor days that the whole company shares, chosen centrally so that teams overlap. Otherwise teams drift into silos, and mixed meetings with half the people on video do not work. And the threat I hear most often, that a company can get cheaper workers elsewhere in the world, is, as Bloom says, [real for fully remote roles](https://www.flexos.work/learn/nick-bloom-stanford-media-hybrid-and-remote-work), which is one more reason to make the office days produce what only presence can. Three answers follow.

The first is about the number. Nick Bloom argues for [coordinated anchor days](https://fortune.com/2023/02/12/organized-hybrid-benefits-commuting-deep-work) on which everyone is in, and a [FlexOS summary of his advice](https://www.flexos.work/learn/hybrid-work-model) describes a company whose two lively office days made people ask for a third. A [randomised trial at Trip.com](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30292), published in Nature in 2024, used fixed anchor days: two days at home a week did not hurt performance or promotions and cut resignations by a third. So the evidence counts against number-only mandates, but fixed anchor days as such are a different matter. The case for team-agreed days rests on fairness and satisfaction. The number should follow what the team needs that month, and if the days are worth it, the team may well ask for more of them.

The second is silos. Team choice does not have to mean every team on its own: a company can set the frame, a minimum or a range, and let each team choose its days inside it, as [Bloom himself accepts](https://www.charterworks.com/hybrid-work-nicholas-bloom-stanford/). The cost is less everyday cross-team contact, so it needs its own occasion, such as a periodic cross-team gathering.

The third is that mixed meetings do not fix themselves. A good room can reduce the friction between those in the room and those on screen, but it does not remove the [imbalance](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/hybridge-bridging-spatiality-for-inclusive-and-equitable-hybrid-meetings/), so discussion-heavy work belongs on the office days and the team agrees on its meeting rules. [Atlassian recommends](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/distributed-work/hybrid-meetings-guide) that if one person is remote, everyone joins from their own screen. I do not follow that rule. In a team spread across locations, someone is always remote, and a colleague who can only join from home, perhaps because of care duties, should not send everyone who travelled in back to their laptops. Hybrid often means three people in a room in one city, five in another and one person at home, and all of them need video and the shared whiteboard described above.

## What your office days are for

Eugene and I want different things from an office: he wants quiet, I want the crowded kitchen. His story convinced me of what is wrong: a count of days that ignores what the days are for and what the office must offer us. Fixing that does not start with a new policy document. It starts with a conversation in each team about which weeks need the room, what happens there, and which work stays at home. The rest of the week then belongs to each of us. Here is a question for your next team meeting: if someone asked your team why it comes in on its office days, what would the answer be?
